Introduction

“Why is my baby suddenly so fussy—even when nothing seems wrong?”

For many parents, teething can feel confusing—especially when discomfort appears without a clear reason. Increased crying, sleepless nights, and constant drooling often leave caregivers wondering what’s normal and what’s not.

Tooth eruption is a natural developmental phase where a baby’s first set of teeth (milk teeth) begin to emerge through the gums. While most babies experience only mild discomfort, the symptoms can vary and sometimes overlap with other health concerns.

Understanding what to expect—and what not to ignore—can make this phase far less stressful.

In this guide, we’ll walk you through the tooth eruption timeline, common symptoms, safe remedies, myths, and the warning signs that may need professional attention.

What Is Teething?

It is the process by which a baby’s primary (milk) teeth begin to emerge through the gums, usually starting with the lower front teeth.

As these teeth push through, the surrounding gum tissue may become slightly sensitive, leading to mild discomfort.

Tooth eruption can affect each baby differently—some may show minimal signs, while others may appear more uncomfortable during this phase.

key facts:

  • Babies have 20 primary teeth in total
  • Tooth eruption occurs in stages and typically continues until around age 3 years

When Does Teething Start in Babies?

Most babies begin teething between 4 and 7 months of age, although the exact timing can vary from one child to another.

Typical eruption order:

  • Lower central incisors – 6–10 months
  • Upper central incisors – 8–12 months
  • Upper lateral incisors – 9–13 months
  • Lower lateral incisors – 10–16 months
  • First molars (upper and lower) – 13–19 months
  • Canines – 16–22 months
  • Second molars – 25–33 months

By around 3 years of age, most children have all 20 primary teeth.

While these timelines are typical, variations of a few months are common and usually not a cause for concern.

Common Teething Symptoms in Babies

For many parents, it can be difficult to tell which signs are normal during teeth eruption and which may need closer attention. A study of 1,100 children found that 68% had at least one teething symptom — so while symptoms are common, not every baby experiences them the same way.

Teething symptoms usually begin a few days before a tooth erupts and tend to improve once it breaks through the gum.

Common signs include:

  • Excessive drooling
  • Swollen, tender, or reddened gums
  • Chewing or biting on objects
  • Irritability or fussiness
  • Mild changes in sleep patterns
  • Slight decrease in appetite
  • Rubbing of the ear or cheek on the affected side

These symptoms are generally mild and temporary, although the experience can vary from baby to baby. If symptoms seem severe, persistent, or unusual, it’s important to consider causes beyond this developmental phase.

How to Care for Your Baby’s Gums and Teeth During Teething

Good oral care should begin even before the first tooth appears. Keeping your baby’s mouth clean during this phase helps prevent early dental problems and builds healthy habits from the start.

Simple care practices:

  • Gently wipe your baby’s gums with a clean, damp cloth after feeds
  • Once the first tooth appears, start brushing twice daily with a soft-bristled age-appropriate baby toothbrush
  • Use a smear (rice-sized amount) of fluoride toothpaste
  • Once teeth have started to erupt, avoid putting your baby to bed with a bottle, especially one containing milk or juice

Establishing these habits early can reduce the risk of Early Childhood Tooth Decay and support healthy tooth development.

How to Soothe Teething Pain Safely

When your baby is uncomfortable, simple, immediate soothing methods can help calm them.

Quick ways to ease teething discomfort:

  • Gently rub your baby's gums with a clean finger
  • Use a clean, cold, damp washcloth for your baby to bite on
  • Comfort your baby with cuddling and a calm routine

What to avoid during teething:

  • Teething gels containing benzocaine should not be used for teething pain in infants and young children
  • Amber teething necklaces (risk of choking and strangulation)
  • Frozen teethers (can be too hard and may injure the sensitive gums)
  • Applying aspirin or alcohol to the gums

Best Teething Remedies: Safe Ways to Soothe Your Baby

These remedies for babies are simple, safe, and commonly recommended to manage discomfort at home.

Teething tools and remedies to try:

  • Chilled (not frozen) teething ring
  • BPA-free silicone teethers
  • Textured teethers designed to massage gums
  • Clean, cold spoons for gentle pressure

For babies who have started solids:

  • Chilled, soft, age-appropriate foods such as banana or peeled cucumber (always supervise your baby while eating)
  • Cold purees or yogurt
  • Soft, cool foods that are easy to chew

Daily care tips during tooth eruption:

  • Keep the chin dry to prevent drool rash
  • Use a barrier cream if needed
  • Maintain a consistent, comforting routine

If symptoms seem unusual or don't ease up, a quick check-in with your pediatric dentist can help.

Does Teething Cause Fever or Diarrhea? Separating Facts from Myths

Baby teething is often blamed for symptoms that may not actually be related.

What teething may cause:

  • Mild gum irritation and swelling
  • Increased drooling
  • A slight rise in temperature (not a true fever). A temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher should not be attributed to teething.

Symptoms that are not typical of teething:

  • High fever
  • Diarrhea
  • Vomiting
  • Persistent cough or congestion
  • Widespread rash

If your baby develops these symptoms, your pediatrician can help rule out other causes.

When Symptoms May Not Be Due to Teething

It’s important not to assume that all symptoms during this stage are caused by tooth eruption. Some signs may indicate an underlying medical condition.

Contact your pediatrician if your baby has:

  • Fever with temperature above 100.4°F (38°C).
  • Diarrhea or vomiting
  • Rash spreading beyond the mouth and chin area
  • Persistent irritability that does not improve
  • Refusal to feed or difficulty swallowing

These symptoms are not typical of teething and should be evaluated for other possible causes.

When Should You See a Pediatric Dentist for Teething?

While most concerns during this stage can be managed at home, certain situations may require a dental evaluation.

See a pediatric dentist if:

  • No teeth have erupted by 18 months (while some variation is normal, this may need evaluation)
  • Teeth appear discolored or have unusual spots
  • Your baby experiences significant discomfort that does not improve
  • You have concerns about your baby’s oral development

It is also recommended to schedule your baby’s first dental visit when the first tooth appears, or no later than their first birthday.

Early dental visits help monitor tooth development, identify potential issues early, and guide parents on proper feeding and oral care practices.

Conclusion

Teething in babies is a natural milestone—but it doesn’t have to feel overwhelming. With the right remedies, gentle care, and an understanding of what’s normal, most babies move through this phase with minimal difficulty.

The key is not just soothing discomfort, but recognizing when something needs attention. When in doubt, a quick check with your pediatric dentist can offer reassurance and keep your baby’s oral health on the right track.
